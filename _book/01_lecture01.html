1.3 Implementation Science

Example: The \(2 \times 2 \times 2\) factorial CRT to prevent maternal mortality. In practice, it is hard to implement the factorial levels over 3, and more than 3 factors.

Interventions for the RR (risk ratio). Maternal mortality and the combined effects measured by relative risks.

Evidence-based interventions reduce mortality by 60%. Access to interventions across regions.

Implementation science: In real life, at full scale.

Evidence-based interventions. Dissemination research and implementation research.

To adopt and integrate evidence-based health interventions.

Clinical and community-based settings.

1.3.1 The difference for I&DR

  • Theory-driven approaches from social science: CFIR and RE-AIM
    • Culture, equipment, science, economics, health, or people?
    • Collaborate with social scientists
  • Mixed methods
    • Need a lot of field research
    • Qualitative and quantitative
  • Adaptation and fidelity
    • Resourceful, complex, multilevel, and adaptive
    • The MOST and SMART designs for combinations
    • Statistical theory for FDR and power
    • Follow the protocol consistently
  • Contextual vs. external validity
    • Different cultures
    • New situations and new problems
  • Economic evaluations and cost-effectiveness
    • There is always a trade-off
  • Sustainability
    • Interventions should be sustainable
  • Diffusion and networks
    • Go beyond the participants
    • Encourage, diffuse, and distribute to others

Note: Prof. Hagman and Science, Spring semester.

Luke Davis for the model and framework for multilevel interventions for policymakers.

1.4 3R

Rigorous / Rapid / Relevant

Policies change all the time, and we need to pick up our pace.

GLP-1 medications, obesity, and cancer: important public health questions can drive our research questions.

The cascade from risk factors to disease.

Blood pressure: screening for diseases through the program, but the health system is just not ready for the problem.

Screen -> initiate -> sustain -> treatment

Implications for design:

  • Group level instead of the individual level
  • Cluster randomization

1.5 Study design

  • Randomized controlled trials on average
  • RCT: individually randomized
  • cRCT: cluster randomized controlled trial
  • SWD: stepped-wedge cluster design
  • Pragmatic trials
  • Pipeline for efficacy: individual randomization in an academic setting
  • Effectiveness: general practice in the real world
  • Implementation: general population
  • PRECIS-2 to rank how pragmatic a study is for PCORI
  • Follow up on patient-reported issues
  • Primary outcomes and primary analysis

Randomly allocated into different arms:

  • Randomized into clusters
  • Everybody in the same cluster gets the same treatment
  • Violation of the IID (independent and identically distributed) assumption
  • RCT: valid p-values

Example in the slides: HIV+ mothers from different wards.Different approaches for the ECHO study. The primary outcome: PMTCT HIV incidence rate is the outcome. Both groups will receive the treatments.

It is difficult to have more than a \(2 \times 2\) factorial design.

Clusters are not the same size. Consider the number of participants within each cluster.

Free country migration: possible travel between wards?

1.6 Stepped-Wedge Cluster RCTs

  • All individuals must eventually receive the beneficial intervention!
  • The “voltage drop”: weaker effects as implementation becomes more pragmatic and less controlled. There can be a disconnect, and we may not see the changes. The strategy must be designed accordingly.

MaxART stepped-wedge randomized trial.

How many clusters do we need? How many people do we need?

  • Intracluster correlation (ICC) is high.
  • Increase efficiency over a parallel design.
  • Logistically challenging to implement interventions across clusters.
  • A parallel design is not always ideal.
  • Tedious in practice: having all clusters start at the same time can be difficult.
  • What if we cannot randomize the implementation?

1.6.1 Quasi-experimental study designs

  • Pre-post design (self as control; time-varying)
  • Difference-in-differences design; controlled before-after
  • Do we have the same risk factors? What is the difference from Case-control.
  • Interrupted time series
  • Regression discontinuity design

Level change or trend change before and after an intervention.

Colonoscopy and health insurance.Colonoscopy is used for colorectal cancer screening and prevention.

  • Efficacy/effectiveness: Does colonoscopy reduce colorectal cancer incidence or mortality?
  • Implementation: How do we increase appropriate screening in real-world healthcare settings?
  • Potential barriers:
    • Access to healthcare
    • Insurance coverage
    • Patient uptake
    • Provider referral
    • Health-system capacity
    • Follow-up after screening

EMR data: electronic medical records database. Prevention methods for affordable medication.

  • MOST
  • SMART
  • LAGO

The three advanced designs.

Paper: The Value Equation: Three Complementary Propositions for Reconciling Fidelity and Adaptation in Evidence-Based Practice Implementation.

Secondary analysis and observational studies.

FRAME and FRAME-IS.

LAGO stages and the delivery system: analyze which components are working and the cost of each component for the next stage. Use prediction to set up the next stage.

1.7 Hybrid designs for effectiveness and implementation

Type I, II, III.

Implementation outcomes without any health outcomes at all.

If this is going to work in getting the intervention to people, we may not need to look at the initiation rates. It will work anyway. No health-related outcomes may be required for the funding.

Clinical effectiveness research -> implementation research

Intervention, effectiveness, and strategy.

2  BIS 629 Week 1: Dissemination and Implementation Research Designs

Author

Randy Jin

3 0. Paper

Brown CH, Curran G, Palinkas LA, et al. (2017).
An Overview of Research and Evaluation Designs for Dissemination and Implementation.
Annual Review of Public Health, 38:1–22.

4 1. Main Purpose

The paper reviews research designs used in Dissemination and Implementation (D&I) science.

The central problem is:

Knowing that an intervention works is different from knowing how to make it work in real-world settings.

Traditional clinical research asks:

  • Efficacy: Could the intervention work?
  • Effectiveness: Does the intervention work under realistic conditions?
  • Implementation: How do we make the intervention work in real-world systems?

Implementation research therefore focuses on:

  • adoption
  • adaptation
  • delivery
  • fidelity
  • scale-up
  • sustainment

5 2. Dissemination vs. Implementation

5.1 Dissemination

Dissemination = targeted distribution of information or intervention materials to a specific audience.

Main question:

How do we get evidence and information to the people or organizations that need it?

5.2 Implementation

Implementation = strategies used to adopt and integrate evidence-based interventions and change practice within specific settings.

Main question:

How do we actually get an evidence-based intervention used successfully in practice?

6 3. The “7 Ps”

The authors describe seven broad types of interventions relevant to D&I research.

P Meaning Example
Programs Organized intervention programs Cognitive behavioral therapy
Practices Specific practices Behavioral management practice
Principles General guiding principles Prevention before treatment
Procedures Standardized procedures Depression screening
Products Tools or technologies mHealth application
Pills Pharmacological interventions PrEP
Policies Rules or regulations Prescription policies

Thus, implementation science is not limited to medications or clinical treatments.

It can study how programs, practices, procedures, technologies, drugs, and policies are introduced into real-world systems.

7 4. Internal vs. External Validity

A major theme of the paper is the difference between traditional clinical trials and implementation research.

Traditional efficacy research Implementation research
Strong emphasis on internal validity Strong emphasis on external validity
Controlled environment Real-world environment
Homogeneous population often preferred Heterogeneous populations/settings
Research staff deliver intervention Real-world providers/organizations
“Can it work?” “How can we make it work?”

Implementation research does not ignore internal validity.

Instead, there is often a trade-off:

Strong experimental control vs. real-world relevance.

Randomized designs may provide stronger causal inference but can sometimes be difficult or inappropriate in real-world organizations.

8 5. Traditional Translational Pipeline

The paper presents the following general pathway:

Preintervention research
        ↓
     Efficacy
        ↓
  Effectiveness
        ↓
Dissemination & Implementation

8.0.1 Efficacy

Question:

Could the program work?

Characteristics:

  • ideal conditions
  • carefully selected subjects
  • highly trained research team
  • strong monitoring
  • high intervention fidelity

8.0.2 Effectiveness

Question:

Does the program work?

Characteristics:

  • realistic settings
  • more heterogeneous participants
  • clinicians/community practitioners may deliver intervention
  • less experimental control

8.0.3 Implementation

Question:

How do we make the program work?

Focus moves from simply testing the intervention toward determining how the intervention can be:

  • adopted
  • delivered
  • scaled
  • adapted appropriately
  • maintained

9 6. EPIS Implementation Process

The paper describes the EPIS model.

Exploration
    ↓
Preparation
    ↓
Implementation
    ↓
Sustainment

9.1 Exploration

Question:

Should this organization adopt the intervention?

Consider:

  • community needs
  • organizational context
  • policy
  • funding
  • provider experience
  • usefulness of intervention

9.2 Preparation

Prepare the system for implementation.

Examples:

  • collaborations
  • policies
  • funding
  • infrastructure
  • training
  • intervention adaptation

9.3 Implementation

Actually deliver the intervention.

Important components:

  • recruitment
  • training
  • monitoring
  • supervision
  • adherence
  • competence
  • fidelity
  • systematic adaptation

9.4 Sustainment

Question:

Can the organization maintain the intervention after initial implementation support or funding ends?

This is especially important because successful initial adoption does not guarantee long-term use.

10 7. Intervention vs. Implementation Strategy

This distinction is extremely important.

10.0.1 Clinical/preventive intervention

The thing intended to improve health.

Example:

Smoking cessation treatment

10.0.2 Implementation strategy

The method used to get organizations/providers to successfully use the intervention.

Example:

Provider training
+
supervision
+
financial incentives
+
implementation support

Therefore:

Intervention
    ↓
Improves patient health

Implementation strategy
    ↓
Improves adoption/delivery of intervention

An implementation trial often tests the implementation strategy, not whether the clinical intervention itself works.

11 8. Process, Output, and Outcome

The authors distinguish three types of measures.

Measure Meaning Example
Process Activities performed by the health system Frequency of supervision
Output Observable service delivery Number of patients receiving medication
Outcome Patient health or health behavior Disease improvement

A key point:

Many implementation studies primarily evaluate process and output, rather than patient health outcomes.

12 9. Three Major Categories of Implementation Designs

The paper organizes implementation designs into:

1. Within-site designs

2. Between-site designs

3. Within- and between-site designs

13 10. Within-Site Designs

These examine changes occurring inside the same organization or system.

13.1 10.1 Post Design

Measure implementation only after the strategy is introduced.

Implementation
      ↓
   Post-test

Advantages:

  • simple
  • inexpensive
  • useful when baseline information is unavailable
  • useful for studying adoption

Limitations:

  • no baseline comparison
  • often no control group
  • weak causal inference
  • external events can explain observed results

13.2 10.2 Pre-Post Design

Measure outcomes before and after implementation.

Pre
 ↓
Implementation
 ↓
Post

Effect:

Change = Post - Pre

Useful for studying:

  • adoption
  • reach
  • utilization
  • policy/guideline implementation

Major limitation:

Changes may result from factors other than the implementation strategy.

13.3 10.3 Multiple-Baseline / Time-Series Design

Instead of measuring only once before and once after:

O O O O | X | O O O O O

where:

  • O = observation
  • X = implementation

Repeated measurements allow investigators to examine:

  • trends before implementation
  • immediate changes
  • sustained effects

This provides stronger evidence than a simple pre-post comparison.

14 11. Between-Site Designs

Different organizations receive different implementation conditions.

Example:

Sites
  |
  +--------------------+
  |                    |
New strategy      Implementation
                     as usual

15 12. New Strategy vs. Implementation As Usual (IAU)

Sites receiving a new implementation strategy are compared with sites continuing their normal implementation practice.

Site A → New implementation strategy

Site B → Implementation as usual

If randomized:

Sites
 ↓
Randomization
 ↓
-------------------------
|                       |
New strategy           IAU

This is usually a cluster-randomized trial because organizations, practices, communities, wards, etc. rather than individual patients are randomized.

16 13. Level of Randomization

A very important principle from the paper:

Randomize at the level where the implementation strategy operates.

Possible levels include:

Health system
     ↓
Organization
     ↓
Clinic
     ↓
Ward
     ↓
Provider
     ↓
Patient

Example:

If feedback is delivered at the ward level, randomization should generally occur at the ward level.

This helps prevent contamination between experimental conditions.

17 14. Head-to-Head Randomized Implementation Trial

Instead of:

Strategy vs. usual care

compare:

Strategy A vs. Strategy B

The clinical intervention stays the same.

Same evidence-based intervention
             |
      ----------------
      |              |
Strategy A        Strategy B

The research question is:

Which implementation strategy produces better implementation?

Possible outcomes:

  • speed
  • quality
  • quantity
  • adoption
  • reach
  • fidelity

18 15. Dosage / Intensity Trials

Implementation strategies can also vary in intensity.

Example:

No training

vs.

Low-intensity training

vs.

High-intensity training + coaching

Question:

Does more intensive implementation support improve implementation?

19 16. Factorial Designs

Factorial designs test multiple implementation components simultaneously.

Example:

Strategy A: Yes / No
Strategy B: Yes / No

creates:

Group Strategy A Strategy B
1 No No
2 Yes No
3 No Yes
4 Yes Yes

This allows estimation of:

  • effect of A
  • effect of B
  • interaction between A and B

The interaction asks:

Does the effectiveness of strategy A depend on whether strategy B is present?

20 17. MOST

MOST = Multiphase Optimization Strategy

Purpose:

Identify the best combination of components in a complex implementation strategy.

There are three phases:

Preparation
     ↓
Optimization
     ↓
Evaluation

20.1 Phase 1: Preparation

Identify candidate implementation components and define an optimization criterion.

Example:

maximize implementation success
subject to cost ≤ $X

20.2 Phase 2: Optimization

Use factorial/fractional factorial experiments to determine which components contribute most.

20.3 Phase 3: Evaluation

Test the optimized implementation package against a comparison condition.

MOST is particularly useful when there are many possible components but testing every possible implementation package is infeasible.

21 18. SMART

SMART = Sequential Multiple Assignment Randomized Trial

SMART allows implementation strategies to adapt over time.

Basic logic:

Initial strategy
      ↓
Is implementation successful?
      ↓
  Yes       No
   |         |
Continue   Randomize
             ↓
       Additional support

Example described in the paper:

REP implementation strategy
          ↓
     Respond?
      /    \
    Yes     No
             ↓
        Randomization
        /           \
External       External +
facilitator    internal facilitator

This allows researchers to develop adaptive implementation strategies.

22 19. Multilevel / Nested Designs

Implementation often occurs at several levels simultaneously.

Example:

Organization
     ↓
Physician
     ↓
Patient

Different strategies can target different levels.

For example:

Physician-level strategy:
provider training

Patient-level strategy:
medication reimbursement

A nested or split-plot design can evaluate:

  • individual effects
  • additive effects
  • synergistic effects across levels

23 20. Rollout Designs

Rollout designs combine within-site and between-site comparisons.

Sites start implementation at different times.

Example:

Time       1    2    3    4

Site A     0    X    X    X
Site B     0    0    X    X
Site C     0    0    0    X

where:

0 = implementation as usual
X = new implementation strategy

The timing of implementation can be randomized.

24 21. Stepped-Wedge Design

The simplest rollout design is commonly called a:

stepped-wedge design

All sites eventually receive the intervention.

Time →

Site A   Control → Intervention → Intervention → Intervention

Site B   Control → Control → Intervention → Intervention

Site C   Control → Control → Control → Intervention

Advantages:

  • everyone eventually receives the strategy
  • useful when withholding implementation is difficult or unethical
  • allows within-site and between-site comparisons

The paper’s Figure 4 illustrates several versions of randomized rollout designs.

25 22. Head-to-Head Rollout

A more complex rollout can compare two strategies.

             Randomization
                  ↓
          -----------------
          |               |
      Strategy X      Strategy Y

Different sites begin X or Y at randomized times.

This combines:

  • randomized timing
  • head-to-head comparison
  • longitudinal follow-up

26 23. Local vs. Generalizable Knowledge

Implementation research can have two different goals.

26.1 Local knowledge

Question:

How can this particular hospital or organization improve implementation?

This is closely related to quality improvement (QI).

26.2 Generalizable knowledge

Question:

What implementation strategy works across organizations/settings?

This is closer to traditional implementation research.

Therefore, design choice depends partly on whether the objective is:

Local improvement
        vs.
Generalizable scientific knowledge

27 24. Randomized vs. Nonrandomized Designs

27.1 Randomized designs

Advantages:

  • stronger causal inference
  • better control of confounding
  • better protection against external influences

Disadvantages:

  • expensive
  • organizationally difficult
  • sometimes impossible
  • sometimes ethically or politically inappropriate

27.2 Nonrandomized designs

Advantages:

  • practical
  • realistic
  • easier to implement
  • sometimes the only possible design

Disadvantages:

  • confounding
  • selection bias
  • weaker causal inference

Thus:

Strong internal validity
          ↕
Real-world feasibility / external validity

is a recurring tension in implementation research.

28 25. Statistical Power

Implementation research often has multilevel data:

Patients
   ↓ nested within
Providers
   ↓ nested within
Clinics
   ↓ nested within
Organizations

A crucial point is that statistical power may depend heavily on the number of units at the highest randomized level.

For example:

10,000 patients
nested in
10 hospitals

does NOT provide the same information as:

10,000 patients
nested in
100 hospitals

when hospitals are the randomized units.

Ways to improve power include:

  • increasing number of clusters
  • matching
  • blocking
  • covariate adjustment
  • mixed quantitative/qualitative evidence

29 26. Non-Adoption and CACE

A common implementation problem:

Randomized to strategy
          ↓
But organization does not adopt intervention

Assignment therefore does not necessarily equal actual implementation.

The paper mentions:

CACE = Complier Average Causal Effect

as one analytical approach for dealing with incomplete adoption.

This is closely related to the problem of noncompliance in randomized trials.

30 27. Causal Inference Challenges

Implementation research creates unusual causal inference problems.

One major issue is SUTVA.

Traditional causal inference often assumes:

One unit’s outcome is unaffected by another unit’s treatment assignment.

But implementation strategies frequently involve:

  • organizations
  • providers
  • networks
  • communities
  • shared policies

Therefore:

Treatment of Site A
        ↓
may influence
        ↓
Site B

Interference and spillover can therefore violate standard SUTVA assumptions.

Implementation systems are also often dynamic, interactive, and cyclical, rather than simple linear treatment → outcome systems.

31 28. Mixed Methods

Implementation research is not purely quantitative.

Qualitative methods can:

  • precede quantitative studies
  • explain quantitative findings
  • identify implementation barriers
  • identify contextual factors
  • complement statistical analyses

Therefore:

Quantitative
     +
Qualitative
     ↓
Mixed-methods implementation research

can provide a more complete understanding of implementation.

32 29. Designs Outside the Traditional Pipeline

The authors also identify several important approaches that are not covered extensively in the review:

32.0.1 Hybrid designs

Simultaneously evaluate:

Effectiveness
     +
Implementation

32.0.2 Quality improvement designs

Focus primarily on:

Local knowledge
+
improving a specific system

32.0.3 Simulation / synthetic experiments

Use computational approaches to investigate implementation systems.

33 30. How to Choose a Design

The paper does not recommend one universally best design.

Design selection should depend on:

  1. Research question
  2. Existing evidence
  3. Whether the goal is local or generalizable knowledge
  4. Community values
  5. Organizational values
  6. Funder expectations
  7. Available resources
  8. Practical opportunities
  9. Feasibility of randomization

Importantly, implementation studies affect real organizations and communities.

Therefore, the scientifically strongest design may not be feasible without stakeholder support.

36 33. Most Important Take-Home Messages

  1. Efficacy, effectiveness, and implementation answer different questions.

  2. Implementation science studies how evidence-based interventions become routine real-world practice.

  3. The implementation strategy is different from the clinical/preventive intervention.

  4. Important implementation outcomes include adoption, reach, fidelity, adaptation, scale-up, and sustainment.

  5. Implementation studies may use randomized, nonrandomized, quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods designs.

  6. The main design families discussed are:

    • within-site
    • between-site
    • rollout / within-and-between-site designs

  7. Advanced designs include:

    • factorial designs
    • MOST
    • SMART
    • multilevel/nested designs
    • stepped-wedge designs

  8. Cluster-level sample size is particularly important because many implementation strategies operate at the organizational level.

  9. Implementation science often involves interference, non-adoption, multilevel structures, and contextual effects, making causal inference more complicated than a conventional individual-level RCT.

  10. There is no single best implementation design. The design must match the scientific question, context, available resources, and stakeholder needs.
